Sentinel One

A single-unit severe weather annunciator you can build on a bench and live with in your own house. Radar-derived storm tracking on top of an independent radio warning receiver that keeps working when the internet, the power, and the computer all fail.

Build time 6–10 h across stages Cost $180–$520 Soldering minimal, through-hole Region United States only
00

Scope and limits

Read this section before spending money. It determines whether you should build any of this.

The gap this fills

Outdoor warning sirens are not designed to be audible indoors, and the National Weather Service says so plainly. Phone alerts fail when the phone is silenced, dead, or the cell network is congested. The gap is a sleeping household at 2am with no dependable indoor annunciation. That is the problem this solves, and every decision below serves it.

What this is not

Do not rely on this alone

This is an uncertified prototype. It does not replace a NOAA Weather Radio, and it does not predict tornadoes. The radar cell tracking is decision support layered on top of official warnings. weatui's own source says it correctly: “Warnings remain the authority.” Keep a commercial NWR Public Alert receiver in the house regardless of what you build here.

An honest comparison, because it should inform how much you spend:

The first three rows are the life-safety core, and a cheap radio already covers them. Everything you gain here sits in rows four through seven. Build this if you want those rows. Not because the radio falls short.

Prerequisites

  • You live in the United States and are within range of an NWR transmitter.
  • You can use a terminal, edit a TOML file, and follow a wiring diagram.
  • You have weatui building from source (cargo build --release).
01

Architecture

The central design rule: the life-safety path does not run on the computer. A Linux box with a filesystem, a network stack, and a pre-release radar crate in it is a fine place for radar imagery and storm tracking. It is a poor place to put the only thing standing between a sleeping family and a tornado.

So the build splits in two. A small microcontroller owns the radio receiver, the siren, and the battery, running bare metal with no filesystem and no network. The Raspberry Pi runs weatui and everything clever. The Pi may request an alarm. The Pi cannot suppress one.

Kernel panic, corrupted SD card, failed update, out-of-memory, an attacker on your LAN, an expired TLS certificate, NOAA renaming an S3 bucket: none of it can stop the siren. The complicated thing fails, the simple thing wakes you up.

Build in stages, each one useful on its own

Do not attempt the whole thing in a weekend. Every stage below ends in a working, testable state, and you should live with each one for a few days before going further. Stages 1 through 3 give you a useful device. Stages 4 and 5 are what make it trustworthy.

S1

Software spine

Objective: a Pi that boots unattended, runs weatui in daemon mode, and executes a script of your choosing the instant a warning lands for your location. No hardware yet. Everything downstream hangs off this hook.

1.1 Operating system

Raspberry Pi OS Lite (64-bit) is the pragmatic choice for a prototype. Flash with Raspberry Pi Imager, and in the imager's advanced options set the hostname, enable SSH with a public key, and configure Wi-Fi. Do not enable password SSH.

If you intend to take this further than a prototype, NixOS is the better answer, and your repo already carries a flake.nix. Declarative config, atomic generations, and rollback-on-failed-boot are exactly what an unattended safety appliance needs. That is a larger detour than a first build justifies.

# after first boot
sudo apt update && sudo apt full-upgrade -y
sudo apt install -y build-essential pkg-config libssl-dev git

# rust, if not building elsewhere and copying the binary over
curl --proto '=https' --tlsv1.2 -sSf https://sh.rustup.rs | sh

1.2 Build and install

git clone <your weatui remote> ~/weatui && cd ~/weatui

sudo install -m 0755 target/release/weatui /usr/local/bin/weatui
sudo useradd --system --home /var/lib/weatui --create-home weatui

Building on the Pi itself takes a while. If you have a faster machine, cross targeting aarch64-unknown-linux-gnu and copying the binary over is considerably less painful.

1.3 Configuration

Two settings do the heavy lifting here. Setting every tier under [alerts.notify] to "none" disables desktop notifications entirely, which is the supported headless path and skips the notification daemon preflight. Pointing every tier under [alerts.scripts] at one script routes all annunciation through hardware.

/var/lib/weatui/.config/weatui/config.toml
[location]
# Use explicit coordinates, not zip. A zip resolves to a centroid that
# may be kilometres from where you actually sleep, and every distance,
# bearing and approach ETA is measured from this point.
lat = 35.2226
lon = -97.4395

[radar]
site = "auto"          # nearest WSR-88D, or an ICAO id like "KTLX"
frames = 12            # history depth for the loop and the tracker
refresh_secs = 120     # polling floor is 30s; volumes arrive every ~4.5 min

[alerts]
poll_interval_secs = 30
stale_after_secs = 600 # a dead feed is announced after 10 minutes

[alerts.notify]
# No desktop daemon on a headless appliance. Hardware does the alerting.
lethal = "none"
severe = "none"
watch  = "none"

[alerts.scripts]
# Absolute paths are required and validated at load. One script handles
# all three tiers and branches on $1.

One behaviour to plan for

send_gap_recovery fires the LETHAL script hook when the alert feed resumes after an outage, regardless of the configured tier levels, because a dead poller is indistinguishable from calm weather. Your siren will sound when the feed comes back. That is correct and deliberate: you were unprotected during the gap and need to know. Handle ALERT FEED RESUMED as a distinct event in your script so it gets its own tone rather than the tornado pattern.

A placeholder script for this stage, replaced with the real one in stage 2:

/usr/local/lib/weatui/annunciate.sh
#!/bin/sh
# Stage 1: log only. Stage 2 replaces this with hardware annunciation.
set -eu
logger -t weatui-alert "TIER=$1 EVENT=$2 ETA=${WEATUI_ETA_MINUTES:-?} \
DAMAGE=${WEATUI_DAMAGE_THREAT:-none} DETECT=${WEATUI_TORNADO_DETECTION:-none}"
sudo install -d /usr/local/lib/weatui
sudo install -m 0755 annunciate.sh /usr/local/lib/weatui/annunciate.sh

The script hook is a privilege surface

weatui already rejects relative script paths at config load, which is the right guard. Add the rest yourself: the script and its directory must be root-owned and not writable by the weatui user. sudo chown -R root:root /usr/local/lib/weatui && sudo chmod -R go-w /usr/local/lib/weatui. Anyone who can rewrite that file gets code execution as your alert daemon.

Prove it
  1. systemctl is-active weatui returns active.
  2. Run the script by hand and confirm it logs: sudo WEATUI_ETA_MINUTES=7 /usr/local/lib/weatui/annunciate.sh LETHAL "Tornado Warning", then journalctl -t weatui-alert -n 5.
  3. Kill the process (sudo systemctl kill -s SIGKILL weatui) and confirm systemd restarts it within about 5 seconds.
  4. Reboot. Confirm the service comes back with no login.
  5. Leave it running for 48 hours. Check journalctl -u weatui --since "2 days ago" | grep -i error before moving on.
S2

The annunciator

Objective: physical noise and light loud enough to wake a sleeping adult through a closed bedroom door, driven from the script hook you just proved.

This is the stage that turns a monitoring tool into a safety device. Give it more care than the radar.

2.1 GPIO assignment

SignalGPIOHeader pinDirectionNotes
Siren1711OutThrough 220 Ω to MOSFET gate, 10 kΩ gate-to-ground
Strobe2713OutSecond MOSFET, identical circuit
Bed shaker2215OutThird MOSFET
Ack button2316InTo ground, internal pull-up enabled

Two ways to destroy a Pi here

Never connect 12 V to a GPIO pin. The Pi's GPIO is 3.3 V tolerant and will die instantly, possibly taking the SoC with it. Always bond the 12 V supply ground to Pi ground; without a common return path the MOSFET gate has no reference and behaves unpredictably. Bench-test the switch with the siren replaced by an LED and series resistor before you connect anything at 12 V.

2.2 Building the driver board

One 5x7 cm perfboard carries all three output channels. Build one channel completely, test it, then repeat twice. Do not build all three and then test, because if something is wrong you will not know which mistake you made.

Identify the parts before you solder anything

Hold the IRLZ44N with the printed face toward you and the legs pointing down. Left to right the pins are Gate, Drain, Source. The metal tab at the back is electrically connected to Drain, which matters: if you bolt that tab to a grounded metal enclosure you have shorted your 12 V rail through the siren. Leave the tab unbolted, or use an insulating shoulder washer.

On the 1N4007 the painted band is the cathode. Diodes fitted backwards is the single most common error in this build, and a backwards flyback diode is a dead short across your 12 V supply the instant you connect the battery.

Assembly, one channel

  1. Solder the MOSFET near one edge of the perfboard, legs through, tab facing outward with clearance around it.
  2. Solder a 220 Ω resistor from the Pi GPIO input pad to the Gate leg. This limits inrush into the gate capacitance and protects the Pi's output driver.
  3. Solder a 10 kΩ resistor from Gate to Source. Read the note below before deciding this one is optional. It is not.
  4. Fit a 2-pin screw terminal for the siren. Wire one side to Drain, the other to the +12 V rail.
  5. Solder the 1N4007 across those same two terminal pins, band toward +12 V. Physically as close to the terminal as you can get it.
  6. Run Source to the board's ground rail with 18 AWG.
  7. Bring the Pi ground in to that same rail. One ground rail, everything lands on it.
  8. Label the terminal. You will have three identical-looking channels in ten minutes.
Why the 10k pulldown is not optional

Pi GPIO pins come up as high-impedance inputs at power-on and stay that way until userspace configures them. That leaves the MOSFET gate floating for the whole boot, and a floating gate drifts. It can partially turn on the MOSFET and sound the siren every time the Pi reboots, during a kernel panic, and while you are trying to debug something at 2am.

The 10 kΩ resistor holds the gate at 0 V whenever nothing is actively driving it. On a device whose entire job is to be silent until it must not be, this single resistor is doing more work than any other part on the board.

Pre-power checklist, battery disconnected

Meter in continuity mode. Every one of these before anything gets connected.

Bench test at low voltage, before 12 V and before the Pi

  1. Put an LED with a 1 kΩ series resistor in the siren terminal, and feed the +12 V rail from a bench supply at 5 V, or from four AA cells. Not 12 V yet.
  2. Touch the GPIO pad to 3.3 V by hand. The LED lights.
  3. Release it. The LED goes out on its own. If it stays lit or fades slowly, your pulldown is not connected.
  4. Only now connect it to the Pi and drive it with gpioset.
  5. Only after that works, swap the LED for the siren and raise the supply to 12 V. Keep one hand near the disconnect.

2.3 The annunciation script

The gate-drive tooling on Pi OS shifted between releases. Confirm what you have before writing the script:

gpiodetect              # lists chips; note the label for the 40-pin header
gpioinfo | head -30     # confirms line numbering
pinctrl get 17          # the Pi-specific alternative, from raspi-utils

On Pi 5 the header is exposed through the RP1, and the chip label has moved between kernel versions. The script below reads the chip from a variable so you can correct it once rather than in six places.

/usr/local/lib/weatui/annunciate.sh
#!/bin/bash
# Hardware annunciation for weatui alert tiers.
#   $1 = LETHAL | SEVERE | WATCH        $2 = event name
set -euo pipefail

CHIP="${WEATUI_GPIOCHIP:-gpiochip0}"   # verify with: gpiodetect
SIREN=17
STROBE=27
SHAKER=22
STATE_DIR=/run/weatui
mkdir -p "$STATE_DIR"

on()  { gpioset -t0 "$CHIP" "$1"=1; }
off() { gpioset -t0 "$CHIP" "$1"=0; }

# Silence window honoured by the ack button handler, but capped: a user who
# hits mute and falls back asleep must not stay unprotected.
if [ -f "$STATE_DIR/silenced_until" ] &&
   [ "$(date +%s)" -lt "$(cat "$STATE_DIR/silenced_until")" ]; then
  logger -t weatui-alert "suppressed during ack window: $1 $2"
  exit 0
fi

pattern() {  # $1 on-ms  $2 off-ms  $3 repeats
  local i
  for ((i=0; i<$3; i++)); do
    on  $SIREN; on  $STROBE; on  $SHAKER
    sleep "$(echo "scale=3; $1/1000" | bc)"
    off $SIREN; off $STROBE; off $SHAKER
    sleep "$(echo "scale=3; $2/1000" | bc)"
  done
}

logger -t weatui-alert "TIER=$1 EVENT=$2 ETA=${WEATUI_ETA_MINUTES:-?}"

# The feed-recovery notification arrives on the LETHAL hook by design.
# Give it its own signature so it is not mistaken for a tornado.
if [ "${2}" = "ALERT FEED RESUMED" ]; then
  pattern 120 380 4
  exit 0
fi

case "$1" in
  LETHAL)
    # Continuous, 3 minutes, unmistakable. Escalate further if the NWS
    # tagged it as a confirmed tornado rather than radar-indicated.
    if [ "${WEATUI_TORNADO_DETECTION:-}" = "OBSERVED" ] ||
       [ "${WEATUI_DAMAGE_THREAT:-}" = "CATASTROPHIC" ]; then
      pattern 1500 200 72
    else
      pattern 900 300 45
    fi
    ;;
  SEVERE) pattern 400 600 20 ;;
  WATCH)  pattern 200 800 3  ;;
esac

2.4 Acknowledge button

The button must silence the current alert and then re-arm on its own. A safety device that can be permanently muted by a half-asleep hand is worse than no device.

/usr/local/lib/weatui/ackd.py
#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""Ack button watcher. Silences annunciation for a bounded window only."""
import time, pathlib, subprocess
import gpiod                      # python3-libgpiod

SILENCE_SECONDS = 600             # 10 minutes, then it re-arms itself
STATE = pathlib.Path("/run/weatui/silenced_until")
CHIP, LINE = "gpiochip0", 23      # verify with gpiodetect

with gpiod.Chip(CHIP) as chip:
    cfg = {LINE: gpiod.LineSettings(
        direction=gpiod.line.Direction.INPUT,
        bias=gpiod.line.Bias.PULL_UP,
        edge_detection=gpiod.line.Edge.FALLING,
        debounce_period=time.timedelta(milliseconds=40))}
    with chip.request_lines(consumer="weatui-ack", config=cfg) as req:
        while True:
            if req.wait_edge_events(timeout=None):
                req.read_edge_events()
                STATE.parent.mkdir(exist_ok=True)
                STATE.write_text(str(int(time.time()) + SILENCE_SECONDS))
                subprocess.run(["pkill", "-f", "annunciate.sh"], check=False)
                print("silenced for", SILENCE_SECONDS, "s", flush=True)
Prove it
  1. Bench-test with an LED before connecting 12 V. Confirm the LED follows gpioset.
  2. Fire each tier by hand and confirm the pattern differs audibly:
    sudo /usr/local/lib/weatui/annunciate.sh WATCH "Tornado Watch"
    sudo /usr/local/lib/weatui/annunciate.sh LETHAL "Tornado Warning"
  3. Confirm the escalated pattern: sudo WEATUI_TORNADO_DETECTION=OBSERVED /usr/local/lib/weatui/annunciate.sh LETHAL "Tornado Warning"
  4. Measure it. Use a phone SPL meter at the far side of a closed bedroom door. You want 75 dB or more at the pillow. If you do not have that, you have a nightlight, not an alarm.
  5. Press the ack button mid-alert. Confirm it silences, then confirm it re-arms after 10 minutes.
  6. Have someone else trigger it while you are actually asleep. Everything above tests a component. Only this tests whether the device does its job, and it is the one people skip.
S3

Power and ride-through

Objective: keep working when the grid does not. Severe weather takes out power, frequently minutes before the threat arrives. A weather alarm that dies with the lights is theatre.

3.1 Sizing

Measured draw for this build: roughly 8 W sustained with the Pi busy, the SDR running, and the display off. The design target is not "run forever," it is 24 hours of full operation, then weeks of degraded operation. That split is what makes the battery sizing work. Twenty-four hours covers essentially any storm event; the degraded mode covers the multi-day outage afterward when you still need warnings.

Lithium safety, not boilerplate

Use LiFePO4, not lithium-ion. It does not go into thermal runaway, it survives 2000+ cycles, and it tolerates the temperature range of a garage or a closet. This device may sit somewhere that hits 60 °C.

Fuse at the battery terminal, not downstream. An unfused lithium pack can deliver hundreds of amps into a shorted wire and will set your wall on fire. Buy a pack with an integrated BMS; do not assemble raw cells for this.

Do not build mains-voltage circuits. Use a sealed, listed wall adapter for the charger and keep everything you construct on the DC side.

3.2 Wiring the power path

Build this in the order given. The sequence exists to make sure nothing is ever energised into an unverified load.

Step one, before anything else

Fit the fuse holder to the battery positive lead, as close to the terminal as the wire allows. Do this before you strip another wire. A 12 Ah LiFePO4 pack will push hundreds of amps into a shorted screwdriver and set your bench on fire. The fuse is the only thing standing between a slipped probe and an actual emergency.

  1. Fuse the battery positive lead. 16 AWG, blade holder, 5 A fuse. Leave the fuse out of the holder for now so the pack is dead while you work.
  2. Set the buck converter with no load attached. Feed its input from the battery, put your meter on its output, and turn the trim pot until it reads 5.10 V. A little above 5 V covers cable drop, and the Pi 5 is unforgiving about undervolt.
  3. Verify it, then verify it again. Power cycle the buck and re-measure. Cheap modules ship at arbitrary voltages and some have pots that drift when the screwdriver leaves.
  4. Only now wire the buck output to the Pi. 18 AWG, as short as practical.
  5. Tap +12 V for the siren board directly off the fused battery line, not off the buck.
  6. Wire the INA219 in on the high side, between the fuse and everything else, so it sees total current.
  7. Bond every ground: battery negative, buck output ground, Pi ground, driver board ground rail. One point, all of them.
  8. Insert the fuse. This is your first real power-on.
The mistake that kills Raspberry Pis

Connecting a Pi to a buck converter whose output you have not measured. Many modules arrive set anywhere from 1 V to their full input voltage. If it happens to be sitting at 12 V, your Pi dies the instant you plug it in, silently and permanently. Measure first, every time, even on a module you set yesterday.

Pre-power checklist, fuse removed

ProbeExpect
Battery + to battery -, through the whole harnessNo continuity
Buck output + to buck output -No continuity
Buck output polarity against the Pi connectorPositive to positive. Check twice.

3.3 Load shedding

When mains drops, stop doing expensive things. Radar volumes are 10–20 MB every few minutes and HRRR GRIB2 decoding is the heaviest workload in the program; both are also the least life-safety-relevant. Your internet is probably down anyway.

#!/bin/bash
# Swap to a battery config when mains drops. Requires two config files.
set -euo pipefail
CFG=/var/lib/weatui/.config/weatui/config.toml

  # INA219 bus voltage: above ~13.2 V means the charger is supplying.

  fi

4.2 Antenna

Antenna quality is the single most common reason a weather radio appears not to work. A quarter-wave at 162.475 MHz is about 46 cm. If you are using the RTL-SDR dipole kit, set each telescopic leg to that length and orient them vertically in a straight line, not as a V; NWR is vertically polarised. Height beats everything else. If you are in a basement or a storm shelter, you need an outdoor or attic antenna and coax, no exceptions.

S5

Independence

Objective: move the siren, the button, and eventually the radio onto a microcontroller that cannot be taken down by anything happening on the Pi. This is the stage that changes the device's character from "useful gadget" to "thing I would rely on."

Up to now, every alert path runs through Linux. A filesystem corruption, a kernel panic, an out-of-memory kill, or a botched apt upgrade leaves you with a silent box. The MCU fixes that. It costs $5.

5.1 Division of responsibility

MCU owns
  • Siren, strobe, shaker outputs
  • Acknowledge button and its timeout
  • Battery voltage monitoring
  • Its own hardware watchdog
  • A liveness timer on the Pi
  • Eventually: SAME decode
Pi may do
  • Request an alarm at a given tier
  • Send a heartbeat every 30 s
  • Report ETA and event text for display
  • Request a self-test

The Pi cannot cancel an alarm the MCU raised itself. That asymmetry is the point of the whole stage.

5.2 Protocol

Deliberately trivial: line-oriented ASCII over UART at 115200 8N1, one command per line, checksum on the wire. A protocol you can debug with a serial terminal at 3am beats an efficient one.

DirectionLineMeaning
Pi → MCUHBHeartbeat. Absence for 90 s means the Pi is dead.
Pi → MCUALARM LETHAL 7Raise a lethal alarm, ETA 7 minutes.
Pi → MCUCLEARClear alarms the Pi itself raised. Ignored for MCU-raised alarms.
Pi → MCUTESTRun the audible self-test.
MCU → PiSTAT armed batt=13.1 ack=0Status, once per second.
MCU → PiEVT SAME TOR 040109MCU decoded a SAME message itself.
MCU → PiEVT PIDEADHeartbeat lost. MCU is now sounding on its own.

5.3 Firmware sketch

Written against the Pico SDK in C, deliberately without an RTOS. No dynamic allocation, no filesystem, one loop.

sentinel-mcu/main.c (excerpt)
#include "pico/stdlib.h"
#include "hardware/watchdog.h"

#define PIN_SIREN   2
#define PIN_STROBE  3
#define PIN_ACK     14
#define HEARTBEAT_TIMEOUT_MS  90000
#define ACK_SILENCE_MS       600000   /* 10 min, then re-arms */

static uint32_t last_heartbeat, silenced_until;
static enum { IDLE, PI_ALARM, OWN_ALARM } state = IDLE;

static void outputs(bool on) {
    gpio_put(PIN_SIREN,  on);
    gpio_put(PIN_STROBE, on);
}

int main(void) {
    stdio_init_all();
    gpio_init(PIN_SIREN);  gpio_set_dir(PIN_SIREN,  GPIO_OUT);
    gpio_init(PIN_STROBE); gpio_set_dir(PIN_STROBE, GPIO_OUT);
    gpio_init(PIN_ACK);    gpio_set_dir(PIN_ACK,    GPIO_IN);
    gpio_pull_up(PIN_ACK);

    /* If this loop ever stops running, reboot in 8 seconds. */
    watchdog_enable(8000, 1);
    last_heartbeat = to_ms_since_boot(get_absolute_time());

    for (;;) {
        watchdog_update();
        uint32_t now = to_ms_since_boot(get_absolute_time());

        parse_uart_commands(&state, &last_heartbeat);

        /* The Pi going silent is itself an alert condition. A monitoring
         * box that stopped monitoring must not fail quietly. */
        if (now - last_heartbeat > HEARTBEAT_TIMEOUT_MS && state == IDLE) {
            puts("EVT PIDEAD");
            chirp_pattern(120, 2000);   /* distinct from any weather alert */
        }

        if (!gpio_get(PIN_ACK)) silenced_until = now + ACK_SILENCE_MS;

        bool suppressed = now < silenced_until;
        outputs(state != IDLE && !suppressed);

        sleep_ms(10);
    }
}

5.4 Rewiring for the MCU

The driver board does not change. Only what drives its gates changes, moving from Pi GPIO to Pico GP. Both run 3.3 V logic, so there is no level shifter and nothing to translate.

SignalWasBecomesNote
Siren gatePi GPIO17, pin 11Pico GP2, pin 4Same 220 Ω and 10 kΩ on the board
Strobe gatePi GPIO27, pin 13Pico GP3, pin 5
Ack buttonPi GPIO23, pin 16Pico GP14, pin 19Button to ground, internal pull-up
Pi transmitPi GPIO14 pin 8 to Pico GP1 pin 2TX goes to RX. Crossed, not straight.
Pi receivePi GPIO15 pin 10 to Pico GP0 pin 1RX goes to TX

  1. Solder headers to the Pico if it came without them. Seat it in a socket rather than soldering it down, so a dead one swaps out in a minute.
  2. Move the three gate wires from the Pi header to the Pico. Leave the driver board alone.
  3. Run the two UART wires crossed. Transmit on one side goes to receive on the other. Straight-through is the classic mistake here and produces a link that looks wired and does nothing.
  4. Bond Pico ground to the same rail as everything else.
  5. Free the serial port on the Pi: sudo raspi-config, Interface Options, Serial Port, no to the login shell, yes to the hardware port. Skip this and the Linux console fights you for the line.
  6. Verify before trusting it: screen /dev/serial0 115200 and confirm the Pico's once-per-second STAT lines appear.
Power the Pico separately

Feed the Pico from its own 3.3 V buck off the battery, not from the Pi's 3.3 V pin. If it draws power through the Pi, then the Pi dying takes the Pico with it, and you have rebuilt the exact single point of failure this stage exists to remove.

Prove it
  1. Send ALARM LETHAL 5 from a serial terminal. The siren sounds.
  2. Send CLEAR. It stops.
  3. The critical test: sudo systemctl stop weatui, wait 90 seconds, and confirm the MCU raises EVT PIDEAD and chirps on its own.
  4. The harder test: pull the Pi's power entirely. The MCU must keep running and stay armed.
  5. Raise an alarm on the MCU, then send CLEAR from the Pi. Confirm the alarm does not stop. If it does, you have rebuilt the single point of failure you were trying to remove.
  6. Hang the firmware deliberately with an infinite loop. Confirm the watchdog reboots it within 8 seconds.

S7

Siren listener

Objective: hear your county's outdoor warning siren and use it to cross-check every other channel. Not to raise the alarm.

7.1 Why this is a cross-check and not an alert source

Get this framing right or the whole stage is wasted effort. Outdoor sirens are activated by your county emergency manager, who is acting on the same NWS warning your device already received over SAME and CAP, usually a minute or two earlier. Siren detection is therefore normally redundant and slower than what you have.

Its value is entirely in the disagreements:

SirenNWS feedWhat it meansAction
HeardActive warning Normal. Two independent channels agree. Display corroboration. Nothing else.
HeardNothing The state this stage exists for. Either your receive chain is broken, or your county activated on a spotter report or a local policy trigger ahead of any NWS product. Raise a SEVERE notice immediately and run the self-test.
Not heardActive warning Expected most of the time. You are probably out of range, or the storm is louder than the siren. Nothing. Do not treat as a fault.
HeardAlert you suspected was spoofed SAME over the air is unauthenticated. An acoustic siren from the right direction is meaningful independent corroboration. Raise confidence, log it.

7.2 Emergency vehicles are the easy problem

The concern is reasonable and turns out to be tractable. Tornado activation in most US jurisdictions is a steady tone held for three minutes. An emergency vehicle is a frequency sweep that passes by in well under a minute. Those are not close.

7.3 The trick that makes it work

Recognise one siren, not all sirens

You are not building a general siren classifier. You are matching one specific siren on one specific pole at one specific bearing from your house. That is a far easier problem.

Better still, most counties test their sirens on a published monthly schedule, commonly the first Wednesday around noon. That hands you a labelled recording of your own siren every month, for free, and simultaneously verifies the detector still works. It is the same gift the weekly radio test gives you in stage 4. Capture the first few, build a spectral template from them, and match against that.

7.4 The real limitation: range

This will often hear nothing, and that is not a fault

Outdoor sirens are engineered for roughly a mile of outdoor coverage in still air. During a thunderstorm the ambient noise floor climbs steeply from rain, wind and thunder, and audibility collapses. The conditions in which you need it are the conditions in which you cannot hear it.

If you are more than a mile from the nearest siren, expect this stage to detect the monthly test on a calm day and very little else. That is still useful, because the monthly test is what keeps the detector honest, but do not build this expecting a reliable storm-time channel. Never let "no siren heard" suppress or downgrade anything.

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""Outdoor warning siren detector. Advisory cross-check only: this never
raises an alarm by itself, it only reports agreement or disagreement with
the NWS feed."""
import numpy as np, subprocess, collections, time, pathlib, logging

RATE, FRAME = 16000, 4096                 # ~3.9 frames/s
BAND = (400, 1600)                        # siren fundamentals live here
HOLD_S = 90                               # sustained tone before we believe it
PITCH_TOL = 0.06                          # 6% drift allowed across the window
SNR_DB = 12                               # above the rolling noise floor
STATE = pathlib.Path("/run/weatui/siren_heard")

hist = collections.deque(maxlen=int(HOLD_S * RATE / FRAME))
noise_floor = None

def dominant(frame):
    """Peak frequency and its SNR over the rolling floor, in the siren band."""
    global noise_floor
    w = np.hanning(len(frame))
    mag = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(frame * w))
    freqs = np.fft.rfftfreq(len(frame), 1 / RATE)
    sel = (freqs >= BAND[0]) & (freqs <= BAND[1])
    band = mag[sel]
    if not band.size:
        return None, 0.0
    peak = float(freqs[sel][np.argmax(band)])
    power = float(np.max(band))
    med = float(np.median(mag))
    noise_floor = med if noise_floor is None else 0.995 * noise_floor + 0.005 * med
    snr = 20 * np.log10(power / max(noise_floor, 1e-9))
    return peak, snr

def verdict():
    """A siren is a tone that is loud, sustained, and refuses to move."""
    if len(hist) < hist.maxlen:
        return False, "collecting"
    tones = [f for f, s in hist if s >= SNR_DB]
    coverage = len(tones) / len(hist)
    if coverage < 0.85:                    # must be near-continuous, not a pass-by
        return False, f"intermittent ({coverage:.0%})"
    arr = np.array(tones)
    drift = (arr.max() - arr.min()) / arr.mean()
    if drift > PITCH_TOL:                  # a wail sweeps; a siren does not
        return False, f"pitch sweeping ({drift:.0%}) - likely a vehicle"
    return True, f"steady {arr.mean():.0f} Hz for {HOLD_S}s"

proc = subprocess.Popen(
    ["arecord", "-D", "plughw:1", "-c1", "-r", str(RATE), "-f", "S32_LE", "-t", "raw"],
    stdout=subprocess.PIPE)

logging.basicConfig(format="%(asctime)s %(message)s", level=logging.INFO)
while True:
    raw = proc.stdout.read(FRAME * 4)
    if not raw:
        break
    frame = np.frombuffer(raw, dtype=np.int32).astype(np.float64) / 2**31
    hist.append(dominant(frame))
    heard, why = verdict()
    if heard and not STATE.exists():
        STATE.write_text(str(int(time.time())))
        logging.info("SIREN DETECTED: %s", why)
        # Cross-check, never a direct alarm.
        subprocess.run(["/usr/local/lib/weatui/siren-crosscheck.sh"], check=False)
    elif not heard and STATE.exists() and "intermittent" in why:
        STATE.unlink(missing_ok=True)
        logging.info("siren ended: %s", why)

Prove it
  1. Record 10 seconds and confirm the waveform is not clipped and not pure hiss: arecord -D plughw:1 -c1 -r 16000 -f S32_LE -d 10 test.wav
  2. Play a 900 Hz tone from a phone speaker near the mic for two minutes. The detector should fire at about 90 seconds.
  3. Play an emergency vehicle wail recording at the same volume. It must not fire, and the log should say "pitch sweeping".
  4. Play a yelp pattern and a hi-lo. Neither should fire.
  5. Catch the monthly test. Confirm detection, and keep the recording as your calibration reference.
  6. Leave it running through a full week of ordinary neighbourhood traffic and count false positives. You want zero. If you get any, raise HOLD_S before touching anything else.

7.7 If you skip this stage

Nothing else depends on it. It is the only stage in this manual whose absence costs you no coverage, because it adds no channel you did not already have. Build it if you want the cross-check and the extra self-test, or if you happen to live within earshot of a pole. Skip it if you do not, and spend the effort on the stage 4 antenna instead, which will do far more for you.

03

Self-test regime

A safety device that fails silently has negative value, because it replaces appropriate worry with false confidence. You are better off with nothing than with a box you trust that stopped working in March.

This section deserves more of your engineering attention than the radar does.

CheckCadenceDetectsMethod
Missed RWT alarmWeekly Antenna disconnected, receiver dead, decoder crashed, out of range NWR transmits a Required Weekly Test. Expect it. No RWT in 8 days means the RF chain is broken and the device must say so loudly. Free, and it proves the whole path end to end.
Siren current senseMonthly Blown speaker, cut wire, failed MOSFET Sounding a dead siren produces a silence indistinguishable from success. Measure current through the shunt during the test and alarm if it is out of range.
Battery load testMonthly Degraded pack Drop to battery under load for 60 s, log the voltage sag. Compare against the baseline you recorded when new.
Feed stalenessContinuous Dead poller, network partition, NWS outage Already built in via stale_after_secs and send_gap_recovery. Escalate to hardware annunciation.
Monthly siren testMonthly Dead microphone, detector regression, drifted threshold If stage 7 is built: your county's scheduled siren test proves the acoustic chain and refreshes the spectral template. Missing two in a row means the listener is broken. Same free-verification trick as the weekly radio test.
Audible chirpDaily Total system death One short chirp at a set time. Its absence is the signal, so pick a time you are reliably home and awake.
Full-volume testMonthly Everything, together The complete pattern at full volume. Schedule it, warn the household, and do not skip it.
Clock sanityHourly Drift breaking alert expiry Compare system time against GPS and NTP. Divergence over 30 s is a fault.
/usr/local/lib/weatui/selftest.sh
#!/bin/bash
# Daily. Escalates to the siren only when something is actually wrong.
set -euo pipefail
FAULTS=()

# 1. Weekly test received recently? Proves the entire RF chain.
if [ -f /var/lib/weatui/last_rwt ]; then
  AGE=$(( $(date +%s) - $(cat /var/lib/weatui/last_rwt) ))
  [ "$AGE" -gt 691200 ] && FAULTS+=("no NWR weekly test in $((AGE/86400)) days")
else
  FAULTS+=("no NWR weekly test ever received")
fi

# 2. Alert daemon alive and polling?
systemctl is-active --quiet weatui || FAULTS+=("weatui service down")
systemctl is-active --quiet weatui-same || FAULTS+=("SAME receiver down")

# 3. Clock sane?
chronyc tracking | grep -q "Leap status.*Normal" || FAULTS+=("clock not synchronised")

# 4. Battery holding?
V=$(python3 /usr/local/lib/weatui/read_ina219.py)
(( $(echo "$V < 12.8" | bc -l) )) && FAULTS+=("battery low: ${V}V")

# 5. Storage healthy?
[ "$(df --output=pcent / | tail -1 | tr -dc 0-9)" -gt 90 ] && FAULTS+=("disk over 90%")

if [ ${#FAULTS[@]} -eq 0 ]; then
  /usr/local/lib/weatui/chirp.sh ok        # one short beep: alive and well
  logger -t weatui-selftest "PASS"
else
  logger -t weatui-selftest "FAULT: ${FAULTS[*]}"
  # A degraded safety device must be as loud about it as a real alert.
  /usr/local/lib/weatui/annunciate.sh SEVERE "SELF TEST FAILED"
fi
Never auto-update during weather

If you add unattended upgrades, gate them on there being no active alert. Rebooting a household mid-tornado-warning to apply a patch is the kind of failure that only happens once.

04

Testing without a storm

You cannot wait for a tornado to find out whether this works. Every path below can be exercised deliberately.

Never transmit on NWR frequencies

Transmitting on 162.400–162.550 MHz is a federal offence and interferes with a public safety service. Do not build a test transmitter, not even at low power, not even into a dummy load you believe is sealed. Every test below injects signal after the antenna, in software.

Path-by-path

PathHow to exercise it
Script hookCall annunciate.sh directly with each tier and with the escalation environment variables set.
weatui alert logicPoint [location] temporarily at coordinates inside an area with an active warning somewhere in the country, and confirm the whole chain fires. Set it back afterwards, and put a reminder somewhere you will see it.
SAME decoderFeed a recorded SAME burst straight into samedec, bypassing RF entirely. The sameold repository ships test samples.
samedec -r 22050 -- ./same-handler.sh < sample.raw
RF chainThe weekly test. There is no substitute and it costs nothing but patience.
Power failoverPull the plug. Repeat under siren load, which is when brownouts show up.
Pi failuresystemctl stop weatui, then pull the Pi's power entirely. The MCU must carry on.
Feed lossBlock outbound traffic: sudo iptables -A OUTPUT -d api.weather.gov -j DROP. Confirm the stale warning fires after stale_after_secs, then unblock and confirm the feed-recovery alert.
Storm cell trackingReplay a historical outbreak from the NEXRAD archive. Your tree already has an ignored test that does exactly this against KTLX for 2013-05-20:
cargo test moore -- --ignored --nocapture
The whole thingHand someone else the trigger and go to sleep. Everything above tests components; only this tests the product.

06

Placement

The best-engineered alarm in the world is useless in the wrong room. Placement is not an afterthought; for this device it is roughly half the performance.

Do
  • Mount centrally, roughly 1.5 m up, with the siren facing the bedroom hallway.
  • Run the antenna to the attic or outside. Height dominates every other factor.
  • Put a strobe and shaker in each bedroom, not just a siren in the hall.
  • Keep the ack button reachable but not from the bed. Standing up wakes you enough to decide properly.
  • Label it. Anyone in the house should know what it is and what to do.
Do not
  • Put it in the basement with a whip antenna and expect reception.
  • Mount it in a closed cabinet. It needs airflow and an unobstructed acoustic path.
  • Place the antenna next to the buck converters or the Pi. Switching noise at VHF is real.
  • Put the only annunciator behind a closed door from where you sleep.
  • Install it in the storm shelter alone. You need warning before you are in the shelter.
07

Legal and safety

Radio

Receiving is unrestricted. Transmitting on NWR frequencies is a federal offence and interferes with a public safety service. Do not build a test transmitter under any circumstances.

Electrical

Do not build mains-voltage circuits. Use listed, sealed adapters and keep your work on the DC side. Fuse the battery at the terminal. Use LiFePO4 with an integrated BMS, and never charge lithium chemistry with a lead-acid profile charger.

If you ever give one away

The moment this leaves your house it stops being a personal project. Selling or distributing a device marketed for life safety brings product liability exposure, and the intentional radiators need FCC Part 15 testing (budget $5–15k). The consumer standard you would be measured against is CTA-2009 “Public Alert”, plus UN38.3 and IEC 62133 for shipping the battery. Build one for yourself. Think hard before building a second one for someone else.

Coverage limits

  • United States only. NEXRAD, NWS CAP, HRRR, and NWR SAME are all US services. None of this works elsewhere.
  • Radar-derived cell tracking understates the speed of fast supercells, because centroid tracking on a large storm creeps. Measured on the 2013-05-20 KTLX archive, the tornado read 16 kt against roughly 30 kt actual. Treat ETA as an order-of-magnitude figure, not a countdown.
  • Set a proper contact User-Agent for api.weather.gov and keep poll_interval_secs reasonable. It is a free public service run for everyone.

The one thing to take away

Build stages 1 through 4 and you will have something that survives grid loss and internet loss and wakes you up. Add stage 5 and it survives its own computer dying. Skip the self-test regime and none of that matters, because you will not find out it broke until the night you needed it.

And keep the cheap weather radio in the closet anyway.
